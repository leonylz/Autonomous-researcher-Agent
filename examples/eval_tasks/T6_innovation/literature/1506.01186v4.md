# Cyclical Learning Rates for Training Neural Networks

- arXiv: 1506.01186
- URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/1506.01186

## Abstract

The learning rate is arguably the most important hyper-parameter for training
neural networks. This paper describes a simple and powerful method — cyclical
learning rates (CLR) — that lets the learning rate cyclically vary between
reasonable boundary values. Training with CLR converges faster than with a
fixed learning rate: instead of a single tuned LR, a bounded periodic schedule
(e.g. triangular or cosine) explores the LR space during training. The method
requires no extra computation and is robust to the choice of boundaries; it also
performs classification on CIFAR-10 without the need for learning rate
decay schedules.

## Key Method

- Triangular (or cosine) LR cycling between lr_min and lr_max over a step size
  (e.g. 2–10 epochs per half-cycle); often lr_max = 10x lr_min.
- Related to SGDR: SGDR = cosine cycle + warm restart; CLR is the more general
  framing and includes the triangular variant.
- Cheap (pure LR bookkeeping) and pairs with any optimizer.

## Why it matters for this project

An alternative LR-schedule method to SGDR already in the library; if SGDR's
restarts are too aggressive on a 30-epoch budget, a single triangular cycle is
the milder variant — and it is citable as its own method [arXiv:1506.01186].

Abstract
It is known that the learning rate is the most important hyper-parameter to tune for training deep neural networks.
This paper describes a new method for setting the learning rate, named cyclical learning rates , which practically eliminates the need to experimentally find the best values and schedule for the global learning rates.
Instead of monotonically decreasing the learning rate, this method lets the learning rate cyclically vary between reasonable boundary values.
Training with cyclical learning rates instead of fixed values achieves improved classification accuracy without a need to tune and often in fewer iterations.
This paper also describes a simple way to estimate “reasonable bounds” – linearly increasing the learning rate of the network for a few epochs.
In addition, cyclical learning rates are demonstrated on the CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 datasets with ResNets, Stochastic Depth networks, and DenseNets, and the ImageNet dataset with the AlexNet and GoogLeNet architectures.
These are practical tools for everyone who trains neural networks.
3.1 Cyclical Learning Rates: The essence of this learning rate policy comes from the observation that increasing the learning rate might have a short term negative effect and yet achieve a longer term beneficial effect.
This observation leads to the idea of letting the learning rate vary within a range of values rather than adopting a stepwise fixed or exponentially decreasing value.
That is, one sets minimum and maximum boundaries and the learning rate cyclically varies between these bounds.
Experiments with numerous functional forms, such as a triangular window (linear), a Welch window (parabolic) and a Hann window (sinusoidal) all produced equivalent results
This led to adopting a triangular window (linearly increasing then linearly decreasing), which is illustrated in Figure 2 , because it is the simplest function that incorporates this idea.
The rest of this paper refers to this as the t ​ r ​ i ​ a ​ n ​ g ​ u ​ l ​ a ​ r triangular learning rate policy.
Figure 2: Triangular learning rate policy. The blue lines represent learning rate values changing between bounds.
The input parameter s ​ t ​ e ​ p ​ s ​ i ​ z ​ e stepsize is the number of iterations in half a cycle.
An intuitive understanding of why CLR methods work comes from considering the loss function topology.
Dauphin et al . [ 4 ] argue that the difficulty in minimizing the loss arises from saddle points rather than poor local minima.
Saddle points have small gradients that slow the learning process.
However, increasing the learning rate allows more rapid traversal of saddle point plateaus.
A more practical reason as to why CLR works is that, by following the methods in Section 3.3 , it is likely the optimum learning rate will be between the bounds and near optimal learning rates will be used throughout training.
The red curve in Figure 1 shows the result of the t ​ r ​ i ​ a ​ n ​ g ​ u ​ l ​ a ​ r triangular policy on CIFAR-10.
The settings used to create the red curve were a minimum learning rate of 0.001 0.001 (as in the original parameter file) and a maximum of 0.006 0.006 .
Also, the cycle length (i.e., the number of iterations until the learning rate returns to the initial value) is set to 4,000 4,000 iterations (i.e., s ​ t ​ e ​ p ​ s ​ i ​ z ​ e = 2000 stepsize=2000 ) and Figure 1 shows that the accuracy peaks at the end of each cycle.
Implementation of the code for a new learning rate policy is straightforward.
An example of the code added to Torch 7 in the experiments shown in Section 4.1.2 is the following few lines:

where o ​ p ​ t . L ​ R opt.LR is the specified lower (i.e., base) learning rate, e ​ p ​ o ​ c ​ h ​ C ​ o ​ u ​ n ​ t ​ e ​ r epochCounter is the number of epochs of training, and l ​ r lr is the computed learning rate.
This policy is named t ​ r ​ i ​ a ​ n ​ g ​ u ​ l ​ a ​ r triangular and is as described above, with two new input parameters defined: s ​ t ​ e ​ p ​ s ​ i ​ z ​ e stepsize (half the period or cycle length) and m ​ a ​ x ​ _ ​ l ​ r max\_lr (the maximum learning rate boundary).
This code varies the learning rate linearly between the minimum ( b ​ a ​ s ​ e ​ _ ​ l ​ r base\_lr ) and the maximum ( m ​ a ​ x ​ _ ​ l ​ r max\_lr ).

In the subsections below, CLR policies are used for training with the CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and ImageNet datasets.

The next step is to estimate the bounds for the learning rate, which is found with the LR range test by making a run for 4 epochs where the learning rate linearly increases from 0.001 0.001 to 0.065 0.065 (Figure 11 ).
This figure shows that one can use bounds between 0.01 0.01 and 0.04 0.04 and still have the model reach convergence.
However, learning rates above 0.025 0.025 cause the training to converge erratically.
For both t ​ r ​ i ​ a ​ n ​ g ​ u ​ l ​ a ​ r ​ 2 triangular2 and the e ​ x ​ p ​ _ ​ r ​ a ​ n ​ g ​ e exp\_range policies, the b ​ a ​ s ​ e ​ _ ​ l ​ r base\_lr was set to 0.01 0.01 and m ​ a ​ x ​ _ ​ l ​ r max\_lr was set to 0.026 0.026 .
