# Deep Residual Learning for Image Recognition (ResNet)

- arXiv: 1512.03385
- URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.03385

## Abstract

Deeper neural networks are more difficult to train; we present a residual
learning framework to ease the training of networks that are substantially
deeper than those used previously. We explicitly reformulate the layers as
learning residual functions with reference to the layer inputs, instead of
learning unreferenced functions. On CIFAR-10, a plain 34-layer net degrades but
its residual counterpart trains well; a 110-layer ResNet reaches ~91.8% on the
CIFAR-10 test set with standard augmentation. The residual formulation is the
foundation of most modern architectures (ResNet, WideResNet, ResNeXt).

## Key Method

- Identity shortcut: `y = F(x, {W_i}) + x` — the block learns the RESIDUAL
  rather than the mapping; skip connections make deep stacks trainable.
- BN-ReLU-Conv order inside blocks (pre-activation variants too); the CIFAR
  variant uses a stem without aggressive downsampling (32x32 input kept until
  the first stride).
- For small models: even 2 residual blocks can stabilize training vs. a plain
  conv stack.

## Why it matters for this project

The architecture-level option: if the 2-conv baseline's capacity/optimization
is the bottleneck, converting the stack to residual blocks (with BN) is a
paper-grounded architecture change — a different dimension from data-side
methods (mixup/CutMix), and the natural small-scale version of the WideResNet
already in the library.

We initialize the weights as in He2015 and train all plain/residual nets from scratch.

We use a weight decay of 0.0001 and momentum of 0.9, and adopt the weight initialization in He2015 and BN Ioffe2015 but with no dropout. These models are trained with a mini-batch size of 128 on two GPUs. We start with a learning rate of 0.1, divide it by 10 at 32k and 48k iterations, and terminate training at 64k iterations, which is determined on a 45k/5k train/val split. We follow the simple data augmentation in Lee2014 for training: 4 pixels are padded on each side, and a 32 × \times 32 crop is randomly sampled from the padded image or its horizontal flip. For testing, we only evaluate the single view of the original 32 × \times 32 image.

Exploring Over 1000 layers.
We explore an aggressively deep model of over 1000 layers. We set n = 200 n=200 that leads to a 1202-layer network, which is trained as described above. Our method shows no optimization difficulty , and this 10 3 10^{3} -layer network is able to achieve training error < < 0.1% (Fig. 6 , right). Its test error is still fairly good (7.93%, Table 6 ).
But there are still open problems on such aggressively deep models.
The testing result of this 1202-layer network is worse than that of our 110-layer network, although both have similar training error. We argue that this is because of overfitting.
The 1202-layer network may be unnecessarily large (19.4M) for this small dataset. Strong regularization such as maxout Goodfellow2013 or dropout Hinton2012 is applied to obtain the best results ( Goodfellow2013 ; Lin2013 ; Lee2014 ; Romero2015 ) on this dataset.
In this paper, we use no maxout/dropout and just simply impose regularization via deep and thin architectures by design, without distracting from the focus on the difficulties of optimization. But combining with stronger regularization may improve results, which we will study in the future.
