# Rethinking the Inception Architecture for Computer Vision (Label Smoothing)

- arXiv: 1512.00567
- URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.00567

## Abstract

Convolutional networks are at the core of most state-of-the-art computer vision
solutions. While depth and width have been the main levers, this paper explores
ways to scale up networks in ways that aim at utilizing the added computation as
efficiently as possible. A key finding is **label-smoothing regularization
(LSR)**: replacing the one-hot training targets with a smoothed distribution
(e.g. 0.9 on the true class, rest spread over the others) reduces overfitting and
improves calibration. On ILSVRC 2012, Inception-v3 reaches 21.2% top-1 error.

## Key Method

- Label smoothing: `y_smoothed = (1 - epsilon) * onehot + epsilon / num_classes`
  with epsilon typically 0.1.
- One line in the loss computation (or a custom CrossEntropyLoss variant).
- Reduces "over-confidence" and improves generalization, especially with
  heavy augmentation.

## Why it matters for this project

Nearly free to add on top of mixup/CutMix; regularizes the label side of the
training objective rather than the data side — a genuinely different dimension.

Abstract
Convolutional networks are at the core of most state-of-the-art
computer vision solutions for a wide variety of tasks.
Since 2014 very deep convolutional networks started to become
mainstream, yielding substantial gains in various benchmarks.
Although increased model size and computational cost tend to
translate to immediate quality gains for most tasks (as long as enough
labeled data is provided for training), computational efficiency and
low parameter count are still enabling factors for various use
cases such as mobile vision and big-data scenarios.
Here we are exploring ways to scale up networks in ways that aim at
utilizing the added computation as efficiently as possible by
suitably factorized convolutions and aggressive regularization.
We benchmark our methods on the ILSVRC 2012 classification challenge
validation set demonstrate substantial gains over the state of the art:
21.2 % 21.2\% top- 1 1 and 5.6 % 5.6\% top- 5 5 error for single frame
evaluation using a network with a computational cost of 5 5 billion
multiply-adds per inference and with using less than 25 million
parameters. With an ensemble of 4 4 models and multi-crop
evaluation, we report 3.5 % 3.5\% top- 5 5 error and 17.3 % 17.3\%
top- 1 1 error.

3.2 Spatial Factorization into Asymmetric Convolutions: The above results suggest that convolutions with filters larger
3 × 3 3\times 3 a might
not be generally useful as they can always be reduced into a sequence of
3 × 3 3\times 3 convolutional layers.
Still we can ask the question whether one should factorize them into smaller,
for example 2 × 2 2\times 2 convolutions.
However, it turns out that one can do even better than 2 × 2 2\times 2
by using asymmetric convolutions, e.g. n × 1 n\times 1 .
For example using a 3 × 1 3\times 1 convolution followed by a 1 × 3 1\times 3
convolution is equivalent to sliding a two layer network with the same
receptive field as in a 3 × 3 3\times 3 convolution (see figure 3 ).
Still the two-layer solution is 33 % 33\% cheaper for the same number of
output filters, if the number of input and output filters is equal.

In theory, we could go even further and argue that one can
replace any n × n n\times n convolution by a 1 × n 1\times n convolution followed
by a n × 1 n\times 1 convolution and the computational cost saving increases
dramatically as n n grows (see figure 6). In practice, we have found that employing this
factorization does not work well on early layers, but it gives very good results
on medium grid-sizes (On m × m m\times m feature maps, where m m ranges between 12 12
and 20 20 ). On that level, very good results can be achieved by using
1 × 7 1\times 7 convolutions followed by 7 × 1 7\times 1 convolutions.
